I am an Officer on the Data Analytics team at the Development Research Initiative (dRi) in Dhaka. Most of my work is survey research, and I take part in every stage of it. I help write the survey questionnaires, turn them into digital forms in SurveyCTO, KoboToolbox, and ODK, keep an eye on data quality while the survey is still in the field, clean the large and often messy datasets that come back, and run the analysis at the end. The studies I work on are carried out with national and international partners such as the World Bank, the University of Oxford, MIT, LSE, Purdue, and BRAC.

Working with these teams every day taught me to write code that is clean and easy to run again, so a colleague, or my own future self, can reuse it without guesswork. That habit led me to build my own tools. I wrote a set of Stata programs that take the slow, repetitive parts out of survey data cleaning, and I built YourRA, a website that transcribes and translates recorded interviews for qualitative research such as in-depth interviews, focus group discussions, and case studies.

The same data I clean for work also pulls me toward my own questions. Using the Bangladesh Demographic and Health Survey (BDHS), I study how families find and pay for health care, and how this is changing as private hospitals and clinics spread. My papers ask why so many births in Bangladesh now happen by caesarean section, how depression after childbirth is tied to a mother's income and education, how a woman's control over her own reproductive choices affects her mental health, and where patients with high blood pressure slip out of care.

I came to this work through a Bachelor's degree in Statistics from Government Titumir College, affiliated with the University of Dhaka, and an early research internship at the BRAC James P. Grant School of Public Health, where I first saw how much careful data can tell us about people's health. That mix of hands-on survey work and my own research is what I enjoy most, and what I want to keep building on.

Research interest

I am drawn to a simple question: how do policies and programmes actually change people's lives? I want to answer it in low and lower middle income countries, using carefully designed surveys and field experiments, and I keep coming back to public health, health economics, and labour.

Health economics

Labour and development

How work, income, and opportunity shape the wellbeing of families in low and lower middle income countries.

Biostatistics and measurement

Survey design, regression and multilevel models for health data, and careful, reproducible analysis.

  1. 1
    From medical necessity to private sector dominance: a two decade evolution of caesarean section determinants in Bangladesh (2004 to 2022)
    Rony, A. R., Hossen, A., Asha, A. S., and Nahin, K. S. A.
    Scientific Reports (Nature Portfolio), 2026.
    Published DOI Journal page

    Caesarean section (CS) is a critical life-saving intervention when medically directed, yet its prevalence in Bangladesh has surged impulsively from approximately 4% in 2004 to nearly 45% in 2022, highlighting a distinctive South Asian pattern of widespread surgical overuse. This study examines the shifting socioeconomic and geographic determinants of CS, specifically investigating the private sector's role in widening inequalities and the transition of surgical birth from a targeted medical necessity to a routine practice dominated by private-sector utilization. We analyzed Bangladesh Demographic and Health Survey (BDHS) data of 26,845 ever-married women, covering the periods 2004–2007, 2011–2014, and 2018–2022. Variables were harmonized and administrative divisions grouped into broader regions to ensure comparability. Using survey-weighted multivariable logistic regression, we identified independent predictors of caesarean section, adjusting for sociodemographic, clinical, and geographic factors while accounting for complex survey design. Weighted CS prevalence increased sharply, rising from 6.8% in 2004–2007 to 39% in 2018–2022. This surge was largely driven by the private sector, where CS rates expanded from roughly 55% in the earliest period to 84% in the most recent. Longitudinal analyses also revealed a notable shift in key determinants: medical indications, initially strong predictors in 2004–2007 (e.g., Multiple Births AOR 9.55), had substantially weaker effects by 2018–2022 (AOR 2.93), indicating growing widespread saturation of CS beyond clinical necessity. The influence of frequent antenatal care similarly declined over time (AOR 9.62 to 4.62), though it remained a significant predictor. Socioeconomic patterns shifted as well; women in the “poorer” wealth quintile moved from having lower odds of CS in 2004–2007 (AOR 0.53) to higher odds in 2018–2022 (AOR 1.45), suggesting increased penetration of surgical delivery into lower-income groups. Geographically, residence in Coastal areas consistently remained protective relative to the Central region. Caesarean delivery in Bangladesh has shifted from a life-saving procedure to a highly prevalent practice, driven predominantly by the expansion of private-sector facilities. An unregulated private sector, rising healthcare contact, and socioeconomic disparities now fuel overuse, demanding stronger regulation, clinical audits, and expanded midwifery-led care to ensure appropriate use.

  1. 2
    Reproductive coercion as a determinant of anxiety and depression among married women in Bangladesh: a nationally representative survey weighted multinomial analysis of the 2022 DHS
    Nahin, K. S. A., Hossen, A., Asha, A. S., Islam, T., and Rony, A. R.
    Under review, 2026. Preprint available.
    Preprint and DOI

    Purpose. Existing research often treats anxiety and depression as isolated outcomes. This study conducts the first nationally representative analysis of their comorbidity among ever-married women in Bangladesh, specifically investigating the “dual burden” of reproductive coercion and structural powerlessness.

    Methods. Data were analyzed from the 2022 BDHS (n = 20,029). Mental health outcomes were classified into a four-category joint variable (Neither, Anxiety-Only, Depression-Only, Comorbid) using validated GAD-7 and PHQ-9 scales. Survey-weighted multinomial logistic regression was employed to estimate Relative Risk Ratios (RRR).

    Results. Among ever-married women, 15% had symptoms of anxiety only, and 4.2% experienced comorbid anxiety and depression. Older women (45–49 years) (anxiety only, aRRR 1.80, 95% CI 1.39–2.31; comorbid, aRRR 2.48, 95% CI 1.58–3.90) and women facing pregnancy pressure (anxiety only, aRRR 1.63, 95% CI 1.28–2.07; comorbid, aRRR 4.07, 95% CI 2.83–5.85) were substantially more likely to experience psychological distress. Conversely, higher educational attainment was protective, significantly reducing the relative risk of comorbidity (aRRR 0.53, 95% CI 0.32–0.87). Significant regional disparities were observed, with elevated risks of comorbidity in Rangpur and Khulna, while history of terminated pregnancy and internalized acceptance of wife-beating were also identified as independent predictors.

    Conclusion. Anxiety and depression are common among married women in Bangladesh, with comorbid conditions driven by compounded reproductive, interpersonal, and structural stressors. Reproductive coercion and limited autonomy emerged as central determinants of severe psychological distress. Interventions addressing women's mental health should move beyond individual-level screening to incorporate gender equity, reproductive autonomy, education, and region-specific strategies.

  2. 3
    Socioeconomic inequality and postpartum depression in Bangladesh: evidence from BDHS 2022
    Islam, T., Rony, A. R., Nahin, K. S. A., Asha, A. S., Hossen, A., and Hossain, M. G.
    Under review, 2026. Preprint available.
    Preprint and DOI

    Background. Postpartum depression (PPD) is a common maternal mental health disorder with significant implications for mothers and children, particularly in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). Nationally representative evidence on postpartum depressive symptoms in Bangladesh has been limited. This study aimed to estimate the national prevalence of postpartum depressive symptoms and examine individual- and community-level determinants using data from the 2022 Bangladesh Demographic and Health Survey (BDHS).

    Methods. We used data from the 2022 Bangladesh Demographic and Health Survey (BDHS), including 3,117 women aged 15–49 years who had a live birth within the 12 months preceding the survey. Postpartum depressive symptoms were measured using the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9), with a score of ≥ 5 indicating the presence of depressive symptoms. Weighted descriptive analyses were conducted, and multilevel mixed-effects logistic regression models with random intercepts for clusters were applied to examine associated factors. Results are presented as adjusted odds ratios (AORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

    Results. The weighted prevalence of postpartum depressive symptoms was 16.17%. Prevalence varied across administrative divisions, ranging from 13.4% in Mymensingh to 18.1% in Rajshahi. In the fully adjusted multilevel model, household wealth index was significantly associated with postpartum depressive symptoms. Compared with women in the poorest quintile, those in the poorer (AOR 0.59, 95% CI 0.39–0.88), middle (AOR 0.62, 95% CI 0.41–0.95), richer (AOR 0.56, 95% CI 0.34–0.90), and richest (AOR 0.48, 95% CI 0.27–0.86) quintiles had progressively lower odds of depressive symptoms. Community education was associated with lower odds in the community-level model but was not statistically significant in the fully adjusted model. No significant associations were observed for maternal age, education, parity, pregnancy intention, antenatal care visits, delivery characteristics, media exposure, place of residence, or community wealth.

    Conclusions. PPD affects about one in six postpartum women in Bangladesh and is strongly patterned by socioeconomic disadvantage, geographic and community context. Integrating mental health screening into routine maternal health services and addressing socioeconomic disparities may help reduce the burden of postpartum depressive symptoms in Bangladesh.

  3. 4
    The hypertension care cascade in Bangladesh: a nationally representative analysis of BDHS 2022
    Nahin, K. S. A., Rony, A. R., Tasnim, S. J., Islam, T., Hossen, A., and Asha, A. S.
    Under review, 2026. Preprint available.
    Preprint and DOI

    Background. Hypertension is a leading modifiable cause of cardiovascular mortality globally. Nationally representative data on the full hypertension care cascade, prevalence, awareness, treatment, and blood pressure control, are lacking for Bangladesh. This study estimated the burden of hypertension and identified determinants of each cascade stage to inform equity-sensitive policy.

    Methods. We analysed data from 13,760 adults aged ≥ 18 years in the biomarker subsample of the Bangladesh Demographic and Health Survey 2022 (BDHS 2022), a nationally representative cross-sectional survey. Hypertension was defined as systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mmHg, diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mmHg, or current antihypertensive medication use. The care cascade was constructed sequentially across four stages: prevalence, awareness, treatment, and blood pressure control among treated adults. All analyses incorporated complex survey design features. Survey-weighted multivariable logistic regression identified independent determinants of each cascade stage, adjusting for age, sex, education, body mass index, diabetes, wealth quintile, and place of residence.

    Results. Hypertension prevalence was 20.9% (95% CI 19.9–21.9%). Among hypertensive adults, 56.7% (95% CI 54.6–58.8) were aware of their diagnosis, 47.2% (95% CI 45.1–49.3) were on treatment, and 44.2% (95% CI 41.3–47.2) of those treated achieved blood pressure control. Advanced age was the strongest predictor of hypertension (adjusted odds ratio [AOR] for age ≥ 65 vs. 18–34 years, 15.77, 95% CI 12.91–19.26). Female sex, overweight (AOR 2.06), obesity (AOR 3.52), diabetes (AOR 1.50), and higher wealth were each independently associated with hypertension. Awareness and treatment were more likely among women, older adults, diabetic individuals, and wealthier quintiles. Higher educational attainment was the sole independent predictor of blood pressure control (AOR 2.09, 95% CI 1.28–3.42; control rates 35.8% vs. 59.4% for no education vs. higher education). Sensitivity analyses confirmed robustness of findings.

    Conclusions. One in five Bangladeshi adults has hypertension, yet fewer than half of those treated achieve blood pressure control. Critical care gaps concentrated among younger adults, men, the less educated, and economically disadvantaged populations reflect deep inequities in hypertension management. Universal screening, subsidised antihypertensive therapy, and strengthened primary care are urgently needed to reduce preventable cardiovascular mortality in Bangladesh.

    Abstract reproduced from the preprint.

Research software

Each of these tools started the same way: a job in a real project was taking too long or going wrong by hand, so I built something to fix it. They now save time on every study I touch.

A Stata toolkit for survey data cleaning Stata commands
The problem

A handful of cleaning jobs come up on almost every survey, and each one is slow and easy to get wrong when it is done by hand.

What it does

Each command takes one of those jobs and does it from a script.

  • recode_oth, recode_nrep, recode_rep: answers typed into an "Other, specify" box often need to be folded back into the main options, and enumerators sometimes type something that is already on the list. Once the answers are translated, these three commands sort them into the right category on their own, using how often each answer appears, so nobody has to recode by hand.
  • exprep: many surveys repeat a block of questions, for example the same questions for every disease a person reports, which leaves the data in an awkward shape. exprep flattens it into a tidy layout with one clean set of variables per item, so 27 diseases become 27 clean sets.
  • multisplit: fixing a "select all that apply" answer normally means fixing its hidden yes or no columns too, one by one. multisplit rebuilds those columns straight from the corrected answer and keeps every label in place.
Why it matters

Cleaning that once took hours of careful, repetitive work now runs in seconds, and it runs the same way every time, so the results are easy to trust and to reproduce.

YourRA, Bangla transcription for qualitative research web app, generative AI
The problem

Qualitative research runs on hours of recorded interviews, and turning them into usable text is slow and costly. Done by hand, a research assistant needs about a day for one hour of audio.

What it does

YourRA is a website where a researcher uploads hours of audio at once and gets back a ready Word document. It gives a Bangla verbatim transcript with an English translation, automatic speaker labels for moderators and participants, timestamps, and a respondent demographic table. It accepts common formats (MP3, WAV, M4A, OGG, MP4). It is built for sensitive research: files are encrypted in transit and at rest, uploads are never used to train AI models, and the researcher chooses when the audio is deleted (automatically within one to seven days). At about 399 taka per hour, it costs far less than manual transcription.

Why it matters

A job that took about a day per hour of audio now takes a small fraction of the time and cost, while the researcher still checks the result. It is made for in-depth interviews, focus group discussions, key informant interviews, and case studies.

A study area map generator for Bangladesh R, Shiny
The problem

Field teams, survey managers, and students often need clean, publication ready study area maps but do not have the GIS training to make them, and producing each map by hand is slow.

What it does

An interactive R Shiny application built on Leaflet, terra, elevatr, and GADM boundaries. Users pick administrative units from national level down to upazila, and the app draws the map with automatic legends, elevation shading, and customisable colour palettes, then exports a high resolution image.

Why it matters

Someone with no GIS background can produce a report ready map in a few minutes, which removes a common bottleneck in preparing field plans and study documentation.
